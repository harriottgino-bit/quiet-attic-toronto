Raccoons in the attic

How you'll know it's raccoons

Raccoons don't do subtle. If several of these sound familiar, you can be fairly confident before anyone inspects.

  • Heavy thumping, walking, or dragging sounds at night — far louder than mice or squirrels
  • Torn shingles, ripped soffits, bent roof vents, or a lifted chimney cap
  • A strong, sour odour — raccoons use one corner of the attic as a latrine
  • Raccoons seen climbing to the roof at dusk, or tipped green bins on collection night
  • In spring: chattering and soft crying — a mother with kits, and the attic goes quiet only when they're asleep
  • Insulation torn up and tunnelled through, visible from the attic hatch

Getting them out, kindly

Raccoon work is squirrel work's stronger, more careful sibling — same humane idea, heavier materials, and one non-negotiable rule: the family leaves together.

1

A careful inspection

The pro locates the den, maps the entry damage, and — from roughly March through July — checks for kits. A mother raccoon will not abandon her young, so any plan that ignores them fails; the plan starts with them.

2

The door, and the reunion box

A reinforced one-way door lets the mother out to feed. The kits are gathered gently and placed in a heated reunion box right outside the entry — she collects them herself and carries them to a backup den, usually that same night.

3

Sealed against strong hands

Raccoons can rip open what stops a squirrel, so vents, soffits, and the chimney get heavy-gauge screening and proper caps. Every entry is closed, not just the one she was using.

4

Cleanup, handled safely

Raccoon droppings can carry roundworm eggs, so attic cleanup is specialist work with proper equipment — not a weekend job with a dust mask. If the den was established, your pro can quote decontamination and insulation replacement.

Raccoon questions, answered straight

Should I go up and have a look myself?

No — honestly, this is the one firm “don't” on this page. A cornered mother is protective and strong, and the latrine corner carries roundworm risk. Everything a pro needs to know first, you can describe from downstairs: when it's loud, where it sounds heaviest, what the roof looks like from the ground. The form asks for exactly that.

What actually happens to the babies?

They stay with their mother — that's the whole design. Kits wait in a warm reunion box by the entry; mum, who knows their voices, moves them one at a time to a den she already has elsewhere. Sealing only happens once the attic is confirmed empty and quiet.

Why my house, though?

Nothing personal, and nothing you did wrong. Toronto's raccoon density is famously high, and a loose soffit, an uncapped chimney, and a tree touching the roof is — to a raccoon — a vacancy sign on a perfect hollow tree. The seal-up step takes down the sign.

Can I wait until the kits grow up and leave?

A litter can stay for months, and the damage, odour, and droppings accumulate the whole time — then autumn denning season starts the cycle again. Handled in baby season, with the reunion method, they leave this week instead. Sooner is both kinder and cheaper.

Thumping at night? Start here

If the noise above your ceiling is heavy — thumping, dragging, or something that sounds far too big for a mouse — and it happens mostly at night, raccoons are the likely visitor. They tear at shingles and soffits to get in, and a mother raccoon will happily raise her babies in your attic through spring and summer.

Tonight, don't go up into the attic, and don't try to chase one out. A cornered raccoon is a stressed raccoon, and a mother with babies won't leave them behind. The kind way out is a one-way door, fitted once any babies are old enough to follow their mother out — then the entry point is sealed properly so they can't come back.

When you call, it helps to say when you hear it, where it sounds loudest, and whether you've seen damage around the roof or a raccoon on the roof at dusk. That's what shapes the visit — and the price is confirmed after inspection, never guessed over the phone.
